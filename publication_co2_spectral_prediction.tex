\begin{abstract}
Microscopic traffic simulators such as SUMO and Aimsun are accurate but too slow for iterative planning and real-time control. We propose a surrogate model, based on the spectral properties of the road network, that estimates simulated urban $\text{CO}_2$ emissions in under $6$~ms. Each directed road network is represented by two weighted matrices whose rows are normalized and damped by $\beta = 0.95$, so that all eigenvalues lie inside the unit disk. From them we compute spectral descriptors (spectral radius, commutator norm, Kreiss constant, pseudospectral radius, $H_2$ norm, link sensitivity), which we combine with traffic demand, topology and fleet electrification into a 47-dimensional feature vector. An XGBoost model trained on 349 SUMO simulations from 65 cities reaches $R^2 = 89.39\%$ on emissions per vehicle (5-fold cross-validation: $86.10 \pm 5.12\%$) and a WAPE of $2.74\%$ (unweighted MAPE: $7.28\%$) on total city emissions, against $13.45\%$ (MAPE: $18.20\%$) for a GCN. On nine held-out cities, the WAPE is $3.06\%$ (MAPE $2.67\%$) on base scenarios and $4.25\%$ (MAPE $4.82\%$) over 18 runs. For a city whose features are precomputed, warm-start inference takes under $6$~ms (more than $10^5$ times faster than SUMO), while building the features for a new city takes under $132$~s.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{U}{rban} road transportation accounts for more than $24\%$ of global energy-related greenhouse gas emissions and remains a primary contributor to municipal particulate matter ($\text{PM}_{10}, \text{PM}_{2.5}$) and nitrogen oxides ($\text{NO}_x$) pollution \cite{IEA2023, UN2022}. Accelerating municipal decarbonization requires urban traffic managers to continuously evaluate complex planning interventions, such as low-emission zones (ZFE), dynamic congestion charging, arterial transit lanes, and adaptive traffic signal coordination \cite{Bell1997, Sheffi1985}. 

Microscopic agent-based traffic simulators such as SUMO (Simulation of Urban MObility) \cite{Krajzewicz2012} coupled with Handbook Emission Factors for Road Transport (HBEFA 3.3) \cite{Keller2017} represent the standard for high-fidelity emissions modeling. By resolving individual car-following dynamics (Krauss model), lane changing, and intersection priority rules at one-second time steps, microscopic simulators realistically reproduce non-linear queue formation and localized shockwave propagation. However, this microscopic resolution incurs severe computational costs: simulating one peak hour of traffic across a medium-sized metropolis ($m \approx 10^4 - 10^5$ road segments, $n \approx 10^3 - 10^5$ intersections, $N_{\text{veh}} \approx 10^5$ vehicles) routinely requires between $45$~minutes and several hours of multi-threaded CPU execution. This computational bottleneck precludes interactive municipal scenario exploration, multi-objective infrastructure optimization, and real-time digital twin actuation.

To overcome this limitation, machine learning surrogates have emerged as a promising alternative. However, prevailing deep learning methodologies face fundamental architectural constraints when applied across disparate urban topologies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scale Confounding}: Total emissions are almost proportional to the number of vehicles ($r = 0.9865$). A model trained on total emissions can therefore reach a high $R^2$ by learning vehicle count alone and ignoring the network structure.
    \item \textbf{Local Message Passing and Graph Symmetry}: Standard graph neural networks such as GCN \cite{Kipf2017} use a symmetric normalization of the graph, and even directed models such as DCRNN \cite{Li2018} or T-GCN \cite{Zhao2020} only pass information between neighboring nodes. On large city graphs, this local message passing can lose global information (over-smoothing). We compare our model with GCN and GAT in Section~\ref{subsec:baselines}.
    \item \textbf{Zero-Shot Generalization}: Black-box neural models trained on a single metropolitan area cannot generalize to unobserved urban fabrics without extensive recalibration.
\end{enumerate}

To overcome these fundamental limitations, this paper introduces a physics-grounded topological graph-spectral surrogate framework. 

\textit{Why Non-Normality in Urban Traffic?}: In a network with only two-way roads, the matrix is symmetric and a disturbance decays at a rate given by the eigenvalues. Urban road networks are directed because of one-way streets and turn restrictions, and directed matrices are often non-normal. In a non-normal system, a disturbance (for example a slowdown at one intersection) can grow for a while before decaying, even if all eigenvalues are inside the unit disk. We use this as an analogy for temporary congestion growth.

By representing urban road networks as asymmetric non-normal transition operators and extracting their non-normal spectral signatures, our framework maps macro-topology, vehicle demand, and powertrain fleet mix to micro-simulation emissions in inference time under $6$~ms.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Non-Normal Matrix Operator Formulation \& Dissipative Stability}: We represent each road network by a directed, weighted matrix whose rows are normalized and damped by $\beta = 0.95$, ensuring $\rho(\tilde{A}) = 0.95 < 1$. This guarantees that the $H_2$ resolvent norm and the discrete Lyapunov equation are well defined, while extracting the one-way link imbalance $\Delta(\tilde{A})$, transient disturbance growth $K(\tilde{A})$, pseudospectral sensitivity $\alpha_\epsilon(\tilde{A})$, and analytical link perturbation sensitivity $S_{\max}$.
    \item \textbf{47-Dimensional Multi-Physics Descriptor Vector}: We construct a structured 47-dimensional feature space integrating kinematic demand, fleet electrification, physical topology, 20 non-normal spectral invariants, and dimensionless cross-interaction ratios that bridge microscopic vehicular kinematics with macroscopic network topology.
    \item \textbf{Target Normalization and Empirical Validation}: By formulating the surrogate on the normalized per-vehicle emissions target ($y = \text{CO}_2 / N_{\text{veh}}$), we decouple structural topology from vehicle volume. Trained on 349 microscopic SUMO simulations across 65 global cities, our XGBoost surrogate achieves $R^2 = 89.39\%$ on normalized emissions (5-fold CV $R^2 = 86.10 \pm 5.12\%$), translating to a macroscopic city-scale WAPE of $2.74\%$ (unweighted MAPE: $7.28\%$, RMSE $= 3,680$~kg) on rebuilt total emissions, outperforming GCNs by $+43.47$ percentage points on $R^2$ (reducing total emission WAPE from $13.45\%$ to $2.74\%$). We present detailed case studies across six global archetypes and quantify feature importance via TreeSHAP analysis.
    \item \textbf{Zero-Shot Generalization and Convex Barycentric Diagnostic}: We validate model transferability across nine unseen metropolises, achieving a macroscopic MAPE of $2.67\%$ on representative base scenarios ($4.82\%$ over the full 18-run multi-scenario cohort). We introduce a 21-dimensional convex quadratic programming barycentric projection \cite{Boyd2004} that quantifies topological proximity to the training convex hull, providing an automated confidence metric for zero-shot deployment.
    \item \textbf{Fast Real-Time Inference for Digital Twins}: We benchmark computational latencies across metropolitan networks with up to $68,000$ raw nodes, demonstrating warm-start inference times under $6$~ms (more than $10^5$ times faster than SUMO) and cold-start network parsing under $132$~s, establishing the surrogate as an operational engine for real-time municipal digital twins.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mathematical Foundation of Non-Normal Graph Operators}




\subsection{Asymmetric Directed Graphs and Edge Impedance Matrices}
An urban road network is modeled as a directed multigraph $G = (V, E)$, where $V = \{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ denotes the set of $n$ road intersections (nodes) and $E = \{e_1, \dots, e_m\} \subseteq V \times V$ represents the set of $m$ directed road segments (edges). We keep only the largest strongly connected component, so that every intersection can be reached from every other one. The adjacency matrices are then irreducible \cite{Horn2013, Berman1994}. Each directed edge $e = (v_i, v_j) \in E$ is characterized by its physical length $L_{ij} > 0$ (meters), legal free-flow speed limit $v_{\text{free}, ij} > 0$ (m/s), number of lanes $c_{ij} \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}$, and storage capacity $C_{ij} = L_{ij} \cdot c_{ij} / l_{\text{veh}}$, representing the number of vehicles that fit on the link when stopped ($l_{\text{veh}} = 7.5$~m per vehicle).

We construct two fundamental adjacency matrices governing topological connectivity and kinematic flow impedance:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Binary Adjacency Matrix} $A_0 \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times n}$:
    \begin{equation}
    (A_0)_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } (v_i, v_j) \in E, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
    \label{eq:binary_A}
    \end{equation}
    Parallel roads between the same two intersections are counted as a single link.
    \item \textbf{Physical Impedance Matrix} $A_w \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{n \times n}$:
    \begin{equation}
    (A_w)_{ij} = \begin{cases} \tau_{ij} \cdot c_{ij} = \dfrac{L_{ij} \cdot c_{ij}}{v_{\text{free}, ij}} & \text{if } (v_i, v_j) \in E, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
    \label{eq:adjacency}
    \end{equation}
    where $\tau_{ij} = L_{ij} / v_{\text{free}, ij}$ is the free-flow travel time of the link. The weight $(A_w)_{ij}$ is this travel time multiplied by the number of lanes (in units of $\text{lane}\cdot\text{s}$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Perron-Frobenius Spectrum and Topological Asymmetry}
Because of one-way streets and turn restrictions, A0 and Aw are in general not symmetric.

For any square matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, we denote its spectrum by $\sigma(M) = \{ \lambda \in \mathbb{C} : \det(\lambda I_n - M) = 0 \}$, and its spectral radius by:
\begin{equation}
\rho(M) = \max_{\lambda \in \sigma(M)} |\lambda|.
\label{eq:spectral_radius_def}
\end{equation}

For a non-negative irreducible matrix $M$, the Perron--Frobenius theorem \cite{Horn2013, Berman1994} states that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\rho(M) > 0$ is a simple eigenvalue of $M$;
    \item it has a positive right eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$ and a positive left eigenvector $\mathbf{w}$ ($M \mathbf{v} = \rho(M) \mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{w}^T M = \rho(M) \mathbf{w}^T$), each unique up to a positive scaling factor;
    \item if $0 \le B \le M$ and $B \neq M$, then $\rho(B) < \rho(M)$ \cite{Horn2013, Berman1994}. In particular, removing a road or lowering its weight strictly decreases $\rho$.
\end{enumerate}

We use $\rho(A_w)$ as a descriptor of how strongly the network is connected through its heavily weighted roads and loops. We do not claim that it measures traffic capacity, which is better described by max-flow/min-cut and by the macroscopic fundamental diagram \cite{Geroliminis2008, Daganzo2008}.

\subsection{Non-Normality, Commutator Norm, and $\epsilon$-Pseudospectra}
A matrix $M$ is normal if $M M^T = M^T M$. Symmetric matrices are normal, so a network with only two-way roads is normal. In directed networks, one-way streets can make the matrix non-normal. Asymmetry alone is not enough, though: a cyclic permutation matrix is asymmetric but normal. We therefore measure non-normality directly.

To quantify the degree of departure from normality, we compute the Frobenius commutator norm $\Delta(M)$ \cite{Henrici1962, Asllani2018}:
\begin{equation}
\Delta(M) = \| M M^T - M^T M \|_F.
\label{eq:commutator}
\end{equation}
For the binary matrix $A_0$, $(A_0 A_0^T)_{ij}$ is the number of common successors of intersections $v_i$ and $v_j$, and $(A_0^T A_0)_{ij}$ is the number of common predecessors. On the diagonal, $(A_0 A_0^T - A_0^T A_0)_{ii} = d_{\text{out}}(v_i) - d_{\text{in}}(v_i)$. So $\Delta(A_0)$ is zero when all roads are two-way and tends to grow with one-way streets \cite{Asllani2018}. Complementarily, Henrici's departure from normality is given by $\operatorname{dep}_F(M) = \sqrt{\|M\|_F^2 - \sum_{i=1}^n |\lambda_i|^2}$ \cite{Henrici1962, Elsner1987}.

Any square matrix can be written $M = U (D + N) U^*$, where $U$ is unitary, $D$ contains the eigenvalues, and $N$ is strictly upper triangular \cite{Horn2013}. $M$ is normal exactly when $N = 0$. When $N$ is large, a disturbance can grow for a while even if all eigenvalues lie strictly inside the unit disk \cite{Trefethen2005}.

To measure how sensitive the eigenvalues are to small perturbations, we use the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum \cite{Trefethen2005}:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_\epsilon(M) = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \|(z I_n - M)^{-1}\|_2 > \epsilon^{-1} \},
\label{eq:pseudospectrum}
\end{equation}
which is the set of eigenvalues of all matrices $M + E$ with $\|E\|_2 < \epsilon$. Its radius $\alpha_\epsilon(M)$:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_\epsilon(M) = \sup \{ |z| : z \in \Lambda_\epsilon(M) \}
\label{eq:pseudospectral_radius}
\end{equation}
tells how far from the origin an eigenvalue can move under such a perturbation. For $\tilde{A}$, $\alpha_\epsilon > 1$ means that a small perturbation can make the system unstable.

\subsection{Dissipative Transition Operators, Kreiss Constant, and Discrete Hardy Spaces}
To obtain a stable operator for every network, we normalize each row of $A$ and multiply by a damping factor $\beta$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{A}_0 = \beta D_{\text{out}, 0}^{-1} A_0, \quad \tilde{A}_w = \beta D_{\text{out}, w}^{-1} A_w,
\label{eq:normalized_A}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\text{out}, 0} = \operatorname{diag}(A_0 \mathbf{1})$, $D_{\text{out}, w} = \operatorname{diag}(A_w \mathbf{1})$ (invertible, since every intersection has an outgoing road), and $\beta = 0.95$.

We set $\beta = 0.95$ for two reasons. First, after row normalization the largest eigenvalue is $1$; multiplying by $\beta$ makes it equal to $0.95$, so all eigenvalues lie strictly inside the unit disk and the system is stable.
Second, $\beta$ models vehicles leaving the network. With a $5\%$ chance of leaving at each intersection, a trip lasts on average $1 / (1 - \beta) = 20$ intersections \cite{Kemeny1960}, which is close to the mean trip length in our SUMO runs (mean $21.4$ edges per vehicle). The role of $\beta$ is similar to the damping factor in PageRank \cite{Langville2006}.

Since $\rho(\tilde{A}) = \beta$ for every network, it carries no information and is not used as a feature. We use instead the spectral gap:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\lambda_{\text{Markov}} = 1 - \frac{|\lambda_2(\tilde{A}_0)|}{\beta},
\label{eq:markov_spectral_gap}
\end{equation}
which is related to how fast a random walk on the network mixes \cite{Levin2017}.

Let $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be the vehicle count at each intersection at step $t$. We model its evolution as $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \tilde{A}_w^T \mathbf{x}_t$. The Kreiss constant $K(\tilde{A}_w)$ \cite{Kreiss1962}:
\begin{equation}
K(\tilde{A}_w) = \sup_{|z| > 1} (|z| - 1) \| (z I_n - \tilde{A}_w)^{-1} \|_2
\label{eq:kreiss}
\end{equation}
measures how much a disturbance can grow temporarily before it dies out. The Kreiss Matrix Theorem \cite{Kreiss1962} bounds the largest growth of the powers of $\tilde{A}_w$ between $K$ and $e \cdot n \cdot K$ \cite{Spijker1991}. With $n = 10^4$ to $10^5$, this upper bound is loose, but when $K \gg 1$, a disturbance can grow temporarily before decaying.

Since all eigenvalues of $\tilde{A}_w$ lie inside the unit disk, the resolvent $R(z, \tilde{A}_w) = (z I_n - \tilde{A}_w)^{-1}$ is well defined for $|z| \ge 1$. We measure its size with the $H_2$ norm \cite{Zhou1996, Bamieh2012}:
\begin{equation}
\| R(\cdot, \tilde{A}_w) \|_{H_2}^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} \|(e^{i\theta} I_n - \tilde{A}_w)^{-1}\|_F^2 d\theta = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \| \tilde{A}_w^k \|_F^2 = \operatorname{Tr}(P),
\label{eq:hardy_lyapunov}
\end{equation}
The second equality follows from Parseval's identity \cite{Zhou1996}, and $P$ is the solution of the discrete Lyapunov equation $P - \tilde{A}_w^T P \tilde{A}_w = I_n$. In plain terms, it measures the total response of the network to small random disturbances at every intersection \cite{Bamieh2012, Siami2016}.

\subsection{Analytical Link Sensitivity and Eigenvalue Perturbation Theory}
To estimate how $\rho$ changes when one road is modified, without recomputing the eigenvalues, we use first-order perturbation theory \cite{Restrepo2006}. For $M = A_w$, if the weight $M_{ij}$ changes by a small perturbation $\delta M_{ij}$, then $\rho$ changes by approximately $(w_i v_j / \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{v}) \delta M_{ij}$, where $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ are the real positive right and left Perron eigenvectors ($M \mathbf{v} = \rho \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}^T M = \rho \mathbf{w}^T$):
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \rho(M)}{\partial M_{ij}} = \frac{w_i v_j}{\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{v}}.
\label{eq:kato_restrepo}
\end{equation}
We define $S_{\max}(M)$ as the largest of these sensitivities over all roads:
\begin{equation}
S_{\max}(M) = \max_{(i, j) \in E} \frac{w_i v_j}{\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{v}}.
\label{eq:max_link_sensitivity}
\end{equation}

Evaluating localized edge sensitivities via \eqref{eq:kato_restrepo} requires only $0.41$~ms per edge, compared to $89.4$~ms for full eigensolver recomputations ($218\times$ speedup) while maintaining relative spectral approximation errors below $0.12\%$ for capacity variations up to $5\%$.


\section{Surrogate Machine Learning Pipeline and Feature Engineering}

\subsection{Explanatory Feature Families (47 Descriptors)}
To capture the multi-physics coupling between network topology, non-normal operator spectrum, traffic demand, and fleet characteristics, we construct a 47-dimensional descriptor vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{47}$ structured into five functional families:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Kinematic Traffic Demand (4 Descriptors)}: Total simulated vehicle volume $N_{\text{veh}}$, mean vehicle dwell time $\bar{T}_{\text{sim}}$, total vehicle-kilometers traveled $L_{\text{tot}}$ (total distance driven by all vehicles in vehicle-km), and network-wide mean legal speed limit $\bar{v}_{\text{free}} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{e \in E} v_{\text{free}, e}$.
    \item \textbf{Fleet Powertrain and Fuel Mix (2 Descriptors)}: Fleet electrification ratio $\text{Fleet}_{\text{elec}} = N_{\text{EV}} / N_{\text{veh}} \in [0, 1]$ (zero-tailpipe emissions) and heavy-duty freight fraction $\text{Fleet}_{\text{HGV}} = N_{\text{HGV}} / N_{\text{veh}} \in [0, 1]$.
    \item \textbf{Physical Road Network Topology (10 Descriptors)}: (1) Intersection node count $n$, (2) directed road segment count $m$, (3) network density $\delta = m / (n(n-1))$, (4) mean node degree $\bar{k}$, (5) degree standard deviation $\sigma_k$, (6) mean edge length $\bar{L}$, (7) total network length $L_{\text{net}} = \sum_{ij} L_{ij}$, (8) speed limit variance across edges $\sigma_v^2 = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{e} (v_{\text{free}, e} - \bar{v}_{\text{free}})^2$, (9) total network lane storage capacity $C_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{ij} C_{ij}$, and (10) planar meshedness coefficient $M = (m_u - n + 1) / (2n - 5)$ on the simplified undirected topology (quantifying cycle redundancy \cite{Barthelemy2011}).
    \item \textbf{Graph-Spectral Descriptors (20 Descriptors)}: 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Unweighted and weighted spectral radii $\rho(A_0)$ and $\rho(A_w)$ from \eqref{eq:adjacency} (2 descriptors).
        \item Markov spectral gap $\Delta\lambda_{\text{Markov}} = 1 - |\lambda_2(\tilde{A}_0)|/\beta$ from \eqref{eq:markov_spectral_gap} (1 descriptor).
        \item Leading non-trivial eigenvalue moduli $|\lambda_2(\tilde{A}_w)|, |\lambda_3(\tilde{A}_w)|, |\lambda_4(\tilde{A}_w)|$ (3 descriptors) and leading singular values $\sigma_1(\tilde{A}_w), \dots, \sigma_4(\tilde{A}_w)$ (4 descriptors) (total 7 descriptors).
        \item Commutator non-normality norms $\Delta(\tilde{A}_0)$ and $\Delta(\tilde{A}_w)$ from \eqref{eq:commutator} (2 descriptors).
        \item Kreiss transient growth constants $K(\tilde{A}_0)$ and $K(\tilde{A}_w)$ from \eqref{eq:kreiss} (2 descriptors).
        \item Discrete Hardy $H_2$ resolvent norms $\|R(\cdot, \tilde{A}_0)\|_{H_2}$ and $\|R(\cdot, \tilde{A}_w)\|_{H_2}$ from \eqref{eq:hardy_lyapunov} (2 descriptors).
        \item $\epsilon$-pseudospectral radii $\alpha_{0.01}(\tilde{A}_0)$ and $\alpha_{0.01}(\tilde{A}_w)$ from \eqref{eq:pseudospectral_radius} (2 descriptors).
        \item Maximum link sensitivity descriptors $S_{\max}(A_0)$ and $S_{\max}(A_w)$ defined in \eqref{eq:max_link_sensitivity} (2 descriptors).
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Non-Linear Cross Interactions (11 Descriptors)}: Ratios combining demand with network size and spectral descriptors: demand density $N_{\text{veh}} / L_{\text{net}}$, lane capacity saturation $N_{\text{veh}} / C_{\text{tot}}$, demand $\times$ Kreiss / capacity $N_{\text{veh}} \cdot K(\tilde{A}_w) / C_{\text{tot}}$, number of combustion-engine vehicles $N_{\text{veh}} \cdot (1 - \text{Fleet}_{\text{elec}})$, demand $\times$ commutator / nodes $N_{\text{veh}} \cdot \Delta(\tilde{A}_0) / n$, velocity dispersion ratio $\rho(A_w) \cdot \sigma_v / \bar{v}_{\text{free}}$, demand $\times$ Kreiss / nodes $N_{\text{veh}} \cdot K(\tilde{A}_w) / n$, dwell shock $\bar{T}_{\text{sim}} \cdot \rho(A_w) / L_{\text{net}}$, meshed demand capacity $M \cdot N_{\text{veh}} / C_{\text{tot}}$, stochastic resolvent load $\|R(\cdot, \tilde{A}_w)\|_{H_2} \cdot N_{\text{veh}} / L_{\text{net}}$, and freight congestion load $\text{Fleet}_{\text{HGV}} \cdot N_{\text{veh}} / C_{\text{tot}}$.
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Analytical Commentary on Descriptor Synergy and Subvector Partition}:
The 47 descriptors are grouped into network topology and spectrum, traffic demand, fleet mix, and cross terms. In particular, the vector partitions into $\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{demand}}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{fleet}}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{cross}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{47}$, where $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{21}$ isolates the 21 normalized structural and spectral descriptors used for the convex barycentric diagnostic.

\subsection{Target Normalization and Scale Decoupling}
In naive regression models targeting raw total carbon dioxide emissions $Y = \text{CO}_2$ (kg), the strong collinearity between vehicle volume and total fuel consumption ($r(N_{\text{veh}}, \text{CO}_2) = 0.9865$) causes optimization algorithms to allocate almost all split importance to $N_{\text{veh}}$. Consequently, topological and spectral features are suppressed as minor residuals, and models trained on total emissions can perform poorly when transferring to cities with identical vehicle counts but radically different bottleneck topologies.

To eliminate scale confounding, we formulate the machine learning surrogate on the normalized per-vehicle target:
\begin{equation}
y = \frac{\text{CO}_2}{N_{\text{veh}}} \quad (\text{kg } \text{CO}_2 / \text{vehicle}).
\label{eq:normalized_target}
\end{equation}

Total urban emissions are reconstructed during inference via the deterministic identity:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\text{CO}}_2 = \hat{y} \cdot N_{\text{veh}} = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \cdot N_{\text{veh}}.
\label{eq:reconstructed_target}
\end{equation}

Dividing by $N_{\text{veh}}$ removes the effect of city size, so the model focuses on emissions per vehicle.

\subsection{Model Formulation and Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}
We use XGBoost \cite{Chen2016} with squared-error loss to learn the surrogate mapping $f_\theta: \mathbf{x} \mapsto y = \text{CO}_2 / N_{\text{veh}}$. The optimal hyperparameters, selected via 5-fold cross-validation over 2,400 candidate configurations, are: maximum tree depth 6, learning rate 0.03, 500 estimators, row and column subsampling ratio 0.8, L1 regularization penalty $\alpha = 0.1$, and L2 penalty $\lambda = 1.0$.

Let $Y_i$ denote the ground-truth total $\text{CO}_2$ emissions (in kg) from SUMO simulation $i \in \{1, \dots, M\}$, and let $\hat{Y}_i = \hat{y}_i \cdot N_{\text{veh}, i}$ be the reconstructed total emissions derived from $\hat{y}_i = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)$.
On the normalized per-vehicle target $y$, model accuracy is evaluated using $R^2$ and $\text{RMSE}_y = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}$.
On total metropolitan emissions, we report $\text{RMSE}_Y$, unweighted Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), and Weighted Absolute Percentage Error (WAPE):
\begin{equation}
\text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \frac{|\hat{y}_i - y_i|}{y_i}, \quad \text{WAPE} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^M |\hat{Y}_i - Y_i|}{\sum_{i=1}^M Y_i} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^M N_{\text{veh}, i} |\hat{y}_i - y_i|}{\sum_{i=1}^M N_{\text{veh}, i} y_i}.
\label{eq:metrics_def}
\end{equation}
Because $\hat{Y}_i / Y_i = \hat{y}_i / y_i$, unweighted MAPE is identical on $y$ and on $Y$. WAPE equals total absolute carbon discrepancy divided by total SUMO reference emissions.

All simulations were executed on a dedicated multi-threaded compute node equipped with dual Intel Xeon Platinum 8380 CPUs (160 threads, 2.30 GHz) and 512 GB DDR4 ECC RAM.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Cross-City Generalization \& Regularized Barycentric Diagnostic}
To evaluate model capability when deployed in a new city without prior SUMO calibration, we conduct zero-shot validation across nine global metropolises: Amsterdam, Bogota, Casablanca, Dublin, Helsinki, Montreal, Seoul, Singapore, and Zurich. Six of these networks (Amsterdam, Bogota, Dublin, Helsinki, Montreal, Zurich) and the diagnostic failure case Guanajuato are completely external graphs with zero simulations in the 65-city training corpus. For the three remaining cities that had initial baseline runs in the database (Casablanca, Seoul, Singapore, representing 8 simulations out of 349), validation was conducted under strict leave-one-city-out (LOCO) isolation, where all data and barycentric training profiles from the target city were removed (training on 62 cities) during training to ensure unbiased zero-shot evaluation.

On representative base peak-hour scenarios (Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot}), the surrogate achieves a volume-weighted WAPE of $3.06\%$ and an unweighted MAPE of $2.67\%$ (RMSE $= 3,199$~kg). The lower unweighted MAPE on this cohort ($2.67\%$) compared to the overall cross-validation dataset ($7.28\%$) is explained by city scale: the zero-shot test cities are medium-to-large metropolises ($14,000$ to $98,000$ vehicles), where relative deviations are smaller and smoother than in low-volume suburban networks.

To check whether a new city is similar to the training cities, we write its feature vector $\mathbf{z}$ as a weighted average of the training cities' vectors, by solving a small quadratic program \cite{Boyd2004}. The full 47-dimensional descriptor vector decomposes into $\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{demand}}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{fleet}}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{cross}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{47}$, where $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{21}$ isolates the 21 structural and spectral features ($n, m, \delta, \bar{k}, \sigma_k, \bar{L}, L_{\text{net}}, \sigma_v^2, C_{\text{tot}}, M, \rho(A_0), \rho(A_w), \Delta\lambda_{\text{Markov}}, |\lambda_2|, \dots, |\lambda_5|, \Delta(A_0), K(A_w), \|R\|_{H_2}, S_{\max}(A_w)$). Before solving the quadratic program, each descriptor in $\mathbf{z}$ is standardized ($z_j \leftarrow (z_j - \mu_j) / \sigma_j$) using feature-wise mean $\mu_j$ and standard deviation $\sigma_j$ calculated on the training cities.

Let $\mathbf{X}_{\text{train}} \in \mathbb{R}^{65 \times 21}$ denote the standardized matrix of training cities. We solve:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{65}} \quad & \| \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{X}_{\text{train}}^T \mathbf{w} \|_2^2 + \gamma_{\text{bary}} \| \mathbf{w} \|_2^2 \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \sum_{j=1}^{65} w_j = 1, \quad w_j \ge 0 \quad \forall j \in \{1, \dots, 65\},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:barycentric_qp}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{\text{bary}} = 10^{-3}$ is a small penalty that spreads the weights over several training cities.

Let $\mathbf{w}^* = (w_1^*, \dots, w_{65}^*)^T \in \mathbb{R}^{65}$ denote the optimal barycentric weight vector solving \eqref{eq:barycentric_qp}. Here, $n_{\text{active}} \in [1, 13]$ is defined as the count of training cities with strictly positive barycentric weights ($w_j^* > 0$). The regularized barycentric reconstruction residual distance $d_{\mathcal{H}} = \| \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{X}_{\text{train}}^T \mathbf{w}^* \|_2$ provides an automated confidence diagnostic for zero-shot deployment. Total $\text{CO}_2$ emissions for any unseen test scenario are inferred directly by evaluating the trained surrogate model $\hat{y} = f_{\text{XGBoost}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}})$, and rebuilt as $\hat{Y} = \hat{y} \cdot N_{\text{veh}}$.

\textit{Analytical Commentary on Zero-Shot Generalization}:
As reported in Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot}, evaluating 9 representative peak-hour base scenarios across the unseen test cities yields a MAPE of $\text{MAPE} = 2.67\%$ ($\text{RMSE} = 3,199$~kg), while the aggregate multi-scenario zero-shot benchmark evaluated across all 18 independent test runs spanning varying demand loads (60\% off-peak to 140\% severe congestion) and EV penetrations yields $\text{MAPE} = 4.82\%$ and $\text{RMSE} = 4,812$~kg. Cities with low regularized residual distances (e.g., Zurich $d_{\mathcal{H}} = 0.029$, Dublin $d_{\mathcal{H}} = 0.038$) achieve sub-$2\%$ relative errors ($+1.57\%$ and $+1.44\%$, respectively). Even for topographically complex megacities like Seoul ($29,450$ nodes, $d_{\mathcal{H}} = 0.074$), the relative error remains tightly bounded at $-3.58\%$. Across the nine test cities, the Spearman rank correlation between $d_{\mathcal{H}}$ and the absolute relative prediction error is 0.90. This is consistent with the regularized barycentric distance $d_{\mathcal{H}}$ serving as an effective automated confidence diagnostic for zero-shot deployment.

\subsection{Computational Latency and Digital Twin Integration}
For real-time municipal traffic control, computational latency is critical. We distinguish two operational regimes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Warm Start (Demand and Fleet Screening)}: For a city whose graph and spectral descriptors are precomputed, a new demand or fleet scenario ($N_{\text{veh}}$, $\text{Fleet}_{\text{elec}}$) only changes the demand, fleet and cross-term features, so evaluation takes under $6$~ms. A change to the road network itself (closing a road, adding a lane) changes the matrices. For $\rho(A_w)$, we can estimate the first-order change with \eqref{eq:kato_restrepo}; the remaining spectral features must be recomputed (cold-start cost, item 2 below).
    \item \textbf{Cold Start (New City Onboarding or Network Redesign)}: For a completely new metropolis or a structural infrastructure overhaul, the system downloads OSM data via Overpass API, builds the graph, computes eigenvalues via sparse ARPACK Arnoldi iteration, and generates all 47 features (under $132$~s for $68,000$ raw nodes).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Future Research Perspectives}

While the graph-spectral surrogate demonstrates high predictive accuracy and computational speedups exceeding $10^5 \times$ for fleet and demand screening, three primary limitations define clear avenues for future research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Traffic Signal Representation}: Traffic signals are not explicitly represented in the adjacency matrices ($A_w$ currently incorporates only link length, lanes, and free-flow speed). Adding signal timing, green splits, and coordination policies (e.g., adaptive split-cycle offset optimization \cite{Papageorgiou2003}) into time-varying operators $A_w(t)$ represents an essential direction for junction-level fidelity.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Demand Generation}: Traffic scenarios were generated via SUMO \texttt{randomTrips} rather than empirical household travel surveys (origin-destination matrices). While random trips provide wide topological coverage across varied density regimes, they do not reproduce asymmetric commuter tidal flows between residential suburbs and employment centers.
    \item \textbf{3D Cartography and Subterranean Topology}: As observed in the Guanajuato case study ($d_{\mathcal{H}} = 0.142$), standard 2D planar OSM cartography fails in multi-level, mountain topographies; a likely explanation, which we did not test, is that subterranean tunnels permit unimpeded travel without intersection stops. Future extensions will incorporate 3D elevation profiles and grade-separated vertical connectivity into the graph operators.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph*{Implementation Details and Reproducibility}
To facilitate scientific reproducibility, the experimental software stack utilizes Python 3.11, SciPy 1.11, OSMnx 1.6 \cite{Boeing2017}, scikit-learn 1.3, PyTorch Geometric 2.3, and XGBoost 2.0.0 \cite{Chen2016}. All microscopic simulations were executed in SUMO 1.18.0 \cite{Krajzewicz2012} over a standard 1-hour peak traffic period (3,600~s simulated duration with a 1.0~s step). Deterministic pseudo-random seeds (\texttt{random\_state = 42}) were enforced across all train/test splits, cross-validation folds, and tree building routines. The 65 cities were split into five groups of cities (grouped cross-validation), so that no city appears in both training and test folds. Folds are labelled by their dominant regions for readability. The folds contain 70, 69, 70, 70 and 70 runs. Code, trained model weights, and sample network operators are available open-source at \url{https://github.com/thocle03/publications}.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper introduced a graph-spectral surrogate framework for real-time estimation of urban traffic $\text{CO}_2$ emissions. By representing each directed road network by weighted matrices whose rows are normalized and damped by $\beta = 0.95$, we ensure that all eigenvalues lie strictly inside the unit disk and that the $H_2$ norm and discrete Lyapunov equation are well defined. From these operators, we compute non-normal spectral descriptors (spectral radius, commutator norm, Kreiss constant, pseudospectral radius, $H_2$ norm, link sensitivity).

Combined with traffic demand, physical topology, and fleet electrification into a 47-dimensional descriptor vector, our vehicle-normalized XGBoost surrogate ($y = \text{CO}_2 / N_{\text{veh}}$) was trained on 349 microscopic SUMO simulations across 65 global cities. The model achieves an internal test $R^2 = 89.39\%$ on normalized per-vehicle emissions (5-fold cross-validation $R^2 = 86.10 \pm 5.12\%$), translating to a macroscopic city-scale WAPE of $2.74\%$ (unweighted MAPE: $7.28\%$, RMSE $= 3,680$~kg) on rebuilt total emissions, outperforming spatial GCNs by $+43.47$ percentage points on model $R^2$ (reducing total emission WAPE from $13.45\%$ to $2.74\%$). On nine unseen validation cities, the model achieves a macroscopic MAPE of $2.67\%$ on base scenarios ($4.82\%$ across the full 18-run cohort), supported by a 21-dimensional convex quadratic programming barycentric projection \cite{Boyd2004} that quantifies topological proximity to training archetypes. With warm-start inference times under $6$~ms (more than $10^5$ times faster than SUMO) and cold-start onboarding under $132$~s, this surrogate provides an operational decision-support tool for municipal decarbonization and real-time digital twins.
