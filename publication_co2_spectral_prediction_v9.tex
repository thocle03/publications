\begin{abstract}
Microscopic agent-based traffic simulators (e.g., SUMO, Aimsun) model urban vehicle interactions and environmental emissions with high physical fidelity, but their super-linear computational scaling renders iterative municipal planning, real-time traffic signal optimization, and exploratory scenario screening prohibitively slow. In this paper, we propose a physics-grounded graph-spectral surrogate framework that estimates simulation-derived urban traffic $\text{CO}_2$ emissions in sub-millisecond inference time. We formulate directed urban road networks as asymmetric, impedance-weighted dissipative transition operators ($\tilde{A}_0, \tilde{A}_w$) with spectral radius $\rho(\tilde{A}) \le 0.95 < 1$, ensuring rigorous mathematical convergence of discrete Hardy $H_2$ resolvent spaces and discrete Lyapunov equations. We extract 20 non-normal spectral invariants, including the Perron-Frobenius spectral radius $\rho(A)$, the Kreiss transient growth constant $K(\tilde{A})$, commutator non-normality $\Delta(\tilde{A})$, $\epsilon$-pseudospectral radii $\alpha_\epsilon(\tilde{A})$, discrete Hardy $H_2$ resolvent norms, and analytical first- and second-order Kato perturbation derivatives. Combined with traffic demand, topological descriptors, and fleet electrification proxies into a 47-dimensional descriptor space, our vehicle-normalized XGBoost surrogate ($y = \text{CO}_2 / N_{\text{veh}}$) is trained on 349 microscopic SUMO simulations across 65 global cities spanning six continents. The model achieves an internal test $R^2 = 89.39\%$ on normalized per-vehicle emissions ($R^2 = 98.95\%$ on rebuilt total emissions) and 5-fold cross-validation $R^2 = 97.99 \pm 0.90\%$, outperforming spatial Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) by $+17.75$ percentage points on total emissions (+43.47 points on normalized emissions) due to immunity from graph over-smoothing and scale-confounding. On nine completely unseen zero-shot validation cities, the surrogate delivers $R^2 = 99.67\%$ across representative base scenarios ($R^2 = 88.66\%$ over the full 18-run multi-scenario cohort), supported by a 21-dimensional convex quadratic programming barycentric projection that quantifies topological proximity to the training convex hull. With warm-start inference times under $6$~ms (speedups $>1.0 \times 10^6 \times$ over SUMO) and cold-start network parsing under $132$~s for metropolises exceeding $100,000$ nodes, this framework provides an operational decision-support surrogate for real-time municipal digital twins.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{U}{rban} road transportation accounts for more than $24\%$ of global energy-related greenhouse gas emissions and remains a primary contributor to municipal particulate matter ($\text{PM}_{10}, \text{PM}_{2.5}$) and nitrogen oxides ($\text{NO}_x$) pollution \cite{IEA2023, UN2022}. Accelerating municipal decarbonization requires urban traffic managers to continuously evaluate complex planning interventions, such as low-emission zones (ZFE), dynamic congestion charging, arterial transit lanes, and adaptive traffic signal coordination \cite{Bell2000, Sheffi1985}. 

Microscopic agent-based traffic simulators such as SUMO (Simulation of Urban MObility) \cite{Krajzewicz2012} coupled with Handbook Emission Factors for Road Transport (HBEFA 3.3/4.1) \cite{Kratzsch2020, Keller2017} represent the gold standard for high-fidelity emissions modeling. By resolving individual car-following dynamics (Krauss model), lane changing, and intersection priority rules at sub-second granularity, microscopic simulators realistically reproduce non-linear queue formation and localized shockwave propagation. However, this microscopic resolution incurs severe computational costs: simulating one peak hour of traffic across a medium-sized metropolis ($n \approx 10^4 - 10^5$ road segments, $N_{\text{veh}} \approx 10^5$ vehicles) routinely requires between $45$~minutes and several hours of multi-threaded CPU execution. This computational bottleneck precludes interactive municipal scenario exploration, multi-objective infrastructure optimization, and real-time digital twin actuation.

To overcome this limitation, machine learning surrogates have emerged as a promising alternative. However, prevailing deep learning methodologies face fundamental architectural constraints when applied across disparate urban topologies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scale Confounding and Volume Trivialization}: In unnormalized regression formulations, total urban emissions scale almost linearly with total vehicle volume ($r(N_{\text{veh}}, \text{CO}_2) = 0.9865$). Standard neural networks minimize global loss by degenerating into volume multipliers, ignoring network topology and failing when density patterns decouple from vehicle count.
    \item \textbf{Symmetric Graph Assumptions and Over-Smoothing}: Classical Graph Neural Networks (GCN, GAT) \cite{Kipf2017, Velickovic2018} rely on symmetric graph Laplacians ($L = D - A$). Real-world urban traffic networks are directed, asymmetric, and non-conservative: traffic flow is strongly convective, producing localized bottleneck queues that propagate directional shockwaves. Message-passing architectures suffer from exponential over-smoothing across multiscale city graphs, failing to capture long-range non-normal convective amplification.
    \item \textbf{Zero-Shot Generalization Failure}: Black-box neural models trained on a single metropolitan area cannot generalize to unobserved urban fabrics without extensive and computationally expensive recalibration.
\end{enumerate}

To overcome these fundamental limitations, this paper introduces a physics-grounded topological graph-spectral surrogate framework. By representing urban road networks as asymmetric non-normal transition operators and extracting their non-modal spectral signatures, our framework maps macro-topology, vehicle demand, and powertrain fleet mix to micro-simulation emissions in sub-millisecond inference time.

\subsection{Key Contributions}
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Non-Normal Matrix Operator Formulation \& Dissipative Stability}: We formulate directed urban road networks as asymmetric, impedance-weighted transition operators ($\tilde{A}_0, \tilde{A}_w$) with an explicit boundary outflow dissipation parameter ($\beta = 0.95$), ensuring $\rho(\tilde{A}) < 1$. This rigorously guarantees the mathematical convergence of discrete Hardy $H_2$ resolvent norms and discrete Lyapunov equations while capturing convective flow vorticity ($\Delta(\tilde{A})$), transient shockwave amplification ($K(\tilde{A})$), $\epsilon$-pseudospectral radii ($\alpha_\epsilon(\tilde{A})$), and analytical first- and second-order Kato perturbation derivatives ($\lambda^{(1)}, \lambda^{(2)}$).
    \item \textbf{47-Dimensional Multi-Physics Descriptor Vector}: We construct a structured 47-dimensional feature space integrating kinematic demand, fleet electrification, physical topology, 20 non-normal spectral invariants, and dimensionless cross-interaction ratios that bridge microscopic vehicular kinematics with macroscopic network topology.
    \item \textbf{Target Normalization and Empirical Validation}: By formulating the machine learning surrogate on the normalized per-vehicle emissions target ($y = \text{CO}_2 / N_{\text{veh}}$), we decouple structural topology from raw vehicle volume. Trained on 349 microscopic SUMO simulations across 65 global cities, our XGBoost surrogate achieves $R^2 = 89.39\%$ on normalized emissions ($R^2 = 98.95\%$ on rebuilt total emissions) and 5-fold cross-validation $R^2 = 97.99 \pm 0.90\%$, outperforming GCNs by $+17.75$ percentage points. We present detailed case studies across six global archetypes and quantify feature importance via TreeSHAP analysis.
    \item \textbf{Zero-Shot Generalization and Convex Barycentric Diagnostic}: We validate model transferability across nine completely unseen metropolises ($R^2 = 99.67\%$ on representative base scenarios, $R^2 = 88.66\%$ over the full 18-run multi-scenario cohort). We introduce a 21-dimensional convex quadratic programming barycentric projection that quantifies topological proximity to the training convex hull, providing an automated confidence metric for zero-shot deployment.
    \item \textbf{Sub-Millisecond Inference for Digital Twins}: We benchmark computational latencies across metropolitan networks with up to $100,000$ nodes, demonstrating warm-start inference times under $6$~ms (speedups $> 1.0 \times 10^6 \times$ over SUMO) and cold-start network parsing under $132$~s, establishing the surrogate as an operational engine for real-time municipal digital twins.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mathematical Foundation of Non-Normal Graph Operators}

\subsection{Asymmetric Directed Graphs and Edge Impedance Matrices}
An urban road network is modeled as a directed, strongly connected multigraph $G = (V, E)$, where $V = \{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ denotes the set of $n$ road intersections (nodes) and $E = \{e_1, \dots, e_m\} \subseteq V \times V$ represents the set of $m$ directed road segments (edges). Each directed edge $e = (v_i, v_j) \in E$ is characterized by its physical length $L_{ij} > 0$ (meters), legal free-flow speed limit $v_{\text{free}, ij} > 0$ (m/s), number of lanes $c_{ij} \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}$, and storage capacity $C_{ij} = L_{ij} \cdot c_{ij} / l_{\text{veh}}$ (vehicles), where $l_{\text{veh}} = 7.5$~m is the average effective vehicle space headway under stopped conditions.

We define two fundamental matrices governing network structure and dynamic flow impedance:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Binary Adjacency Matrix} $A_0 \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times n}$:
    \begin{equation}
    (A_0)_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } (v_i, v_j) \in E, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
    \label{eq:binary_A}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Physical Impedance Matrix} $A_w \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{n \times n}$:
    \begin{equation}
    (A_w)_{ij} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{c_{ij} \cdot v_{\text{free}, ij}}{L_{ij}} = \dfrac{1}{\tau_{ij}} & \text{if } (v_i, v_j) \in E, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
    \label{eq:adjacency}
    \end{equation}
    where $\tau_{ij} = L_{ij} / (c_{ij} v_{\text{free}, ij})$ represents the free-flow traversal time normalized by lane throughput capacity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Perron-Frobenius Spectrum and Topological Asymmetry}
Because urban road graphs contain one-way streets, dual carriageways with asymmetric intersection turn prohibitions, and directional lane allocations, the adjacency matrices $A_0$ and $A_w$ are intrinsically asymmetric: $A_0 \neq A_0^T$ and $A_w \neq A_w^T$.

For a non-negative, irreducible matrix $A_0 \ge 0$ (which corresponds to a strongly connected directed road network), the Perron-Frobenius Theorem establishes fundamental spectral properties \cite{Perron1907, Frobenius1912, Cvetkovic1980}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The spectral radius $\rho(A_0) = \max_i |\lambda_i(A_0)|$ is a simple, strictly positive real eigenvalue ($\rho(A_0) > 0$).
    \item There exists a unique, strictly positive right eigenvector $\mathbf{v} > 0$ (the Perron vector) such that $A_0 \mathbf{v} = \rho(A_0) \mathbf{v}$.
    \item For any other eigenvalue $\lambda \in \sigma(A_0)$, $|\lambda| \le \rho(A_0)$.
\end{enumerate}

In urban transportation physics, the Perron-Frobenius spectral radius $\rho(A_w)$ quantifies the global asymptotic throughput capacity of the network under free-flow conditions, serving as a macroscopic upper bound on vehicular circulation rate.

\subsection{Non-Normality, Commutator Norm, and $\epsilon$-Pseudospectra}
A matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is normal if and only if it commutes with its transpose: $A A^T = A^T A$. While undirected road networks yield symmetric (and therefore normal) graph Laplacians, real-world directed road networks are highly non-normal ($A A^T \neq A^T A$).

To quantify the degree of structural non-normality, we compute the Frobenius commutator norm $\Delta(A)$ \cite{Henrici1962}:
\begin{equation}
\Delta(A) = \| A A^T - A^T A \|_F = \sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}\left( (A A^T - A^T A)(A A^T - A^T A)^T \right)}.
\label{eq:commutator}
\end{equation}

By Schur\'s unitary triangularization theorem \cite{Schur1909}, any square matrix $A_w \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ can be decomposed as $A_w = U (D + N) U^*$, where $U$ is unitary, $D$ is diagonal containing the eigenvalues $\lambda_i(A_w)$, and $N$ is strictly upper triangular. The matrix is normal if and only if $N = 0$. In non-normal matrices ($N \neq 0$), the eigenvectors are non-orthogonal, leading to transient dynamical phenomena that cannot be predicted by eigenvalues alone \cite{Trefethen2005}.

In hydrodynamic traffic flow theory \cite{Lighthill1955, Richards1956, Kerner2009, Whitham2011}, stop-and-go waves propagate backwards against the traffic stream. In linear dynamical systems $\dot{x} = A_w x$, non-normality enables massive transient growth $\|e^{t A_w}\| \gg 1$ even when all eigenvalues have negative real parts ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$).

To capture this transient vulnerability, we introduce the $\epsilon$-pseudospectrum $\Lambda_\epsilon(A_w)$ \cite{Trefethen2005}:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_\epsilon(A_w) = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \|(z I - A_w)^{-1}\| > \epsilon^{-1} \},
\label{eq:pseudospectrum}
\end{equation}
which represents the set of eigenvalues of all perturbed matrices $A_w + E$ with $\|E\| < \epsilon$. The $\epsilon$-pseudospectral radius $\alpha_\epsilon(\tilde{A}_w) = \sup \{ |z| : z \in \Lambda_\epsilon(\tilde{A}_w) \}$ governs short-term congestion amplification before asymptotic dissipation.

\textit{Analytical Commentary on Hydrodynamic Analogues}:
As detailed in Table~\ref{tab:operator_hydro}, each non-normal spectral metric corresponds directly to a distinct macroscopic traffic phenomenon. While classical graph Laplacians assume symmetric diffusive spreading, urban traffic is strictly directional and convective. The commutator norm $\Delta(\tilde{A}_0)$ measures structural asymmetry: in cities with complex one-way grids (e.g., Paris, Manhattan), vehicular streams cannot simply reverse direction, creating rotational vorticity and elevated intersection shearing. The Kreiss constant $K(\tilde{A}_w)$ bounds the peak transient amplification of perturbations; high $K$ values identify networks where localized braking maneuvers rapidly escalate into city-wide stop-and-go shockwaves, generating severe fuel consumption spikes in HBEFA microscopic emission models.

\subsection{Dissipative Transition Operators, Kreiss Constant, and Discrete Hardy Spaces}
To ensure rigorous mathematical stability across networks of varying sizes ($n = 10^3$ to $10^5$ nodes) while guaranteeing the mathematical convergence of discrete Hardy norms and Lyapunov equations, we construct dissipative transition operators $\tilde{A}_0$ and $\tilde{A}_w$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{A}_0 = \beta D_{\text{out}, 0}^{-1} A_0, \quad \tilde{A}_w = \beta D_{\text{out}, w}^{-1} A_w,
\label{eq:normalized_A}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\text{out}, 0} = \operatorname{diag}(A_0 \mathbf{1})$, $D_{\text{out}, w} = \operatorname{diag}(A_w \mathbf{1})$, and $\beta = 1 - \epsilon_{\text{stab}} = 0.95 \in (0, 1)$ is a physical boundary outflow dissipation parameter. In open urban networks, vehicular flow is inherently dissipative: vehicles exit the network boundaries, park, or reach trip destinations, inducing an effective boundary leakage of $1 - \beta = 0.05$ per step. Consequently, the transition operator is strictly substochastic with spectral radius:
\begin{equation}
\rho(\tilde{A}_0) \le \beta = 0.95 < 1, \quad \rho(\tilde{A}_w) \le \beta = 0.95 < 1.
\label{eq:spectral_radius_beta}
\end{equation}

For any node with zero out-degree (sinks), the corresponding diagonal entry is set to $1$ before normalization to prevent singularity.

The discrete-time dynamical evolution of vehicular density $x_{t+1} = \tilde{A}_w x_t$ is governed by the Kreiss Matrix Theorem \cite{Kreiss1962}:
\begin{equation}
K(\tilde{A}_w) = \sup_{|z| > 1} (|z| - 1) \| (z I - \tilde{A}_w)^{-1} \|.
\label{eq:kreiss}
\end{equation}

The Kreiss constant satisfies the two-sided inequality:
\begin{equation}
K(\tilde{A}_w) \le \sup_{k \ge 0} \| \tilde{A}_w^k \| \le e \cdot n \cdot K(\tilde{A}_w).
\label{eq:kreiss_bound}
\end{equation}

When $K(\tilde{A}_w) \gg 1$, vehicular perturbations experience massive transient growth before decaying to steady-state equilibrium, strongly driving micro-acceleration emission bursts.

Because $\rho(\tilde{A}_w) < 1$, the resolvent operator $(e^{i\theta} I - \tilde{A}_w)^{-1}$ is regular and analytic on the closed unit circle $\mathbb{T} = \{e^{i\theta} : \theta \in [0, 2\pi)\}$. We project the resolvent onto the discrete Hardy space $H_2(\mathbb{D}^c)$ over the exterior of the unit disk:
\begin{equation}
\| R(z, \tilde{A}_w) \|_{H_2}^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} \operatorname{Tr}\left( \left((e^{i\theta} I - \tilde{A}_w)^{-1}\right)^* (e^{i\theta} I - \tilde{A}_w)^{-1} \right) d\theta.
\label{eq:hardy}
\end{equation}

By expanding the resolvent in Laurent series $(e^{i\theta} I - \tilde{A}_w)^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty e^{-i(k+1)\theta} \tilde{A}_w^k$ and applying Parseval\'s identity \cite{Hardy1915, Trefethen2005}, the integral converges unconditionally to the discrete Lyapunov sum:
\begin{equation}
\| R(z, \tilde{A}_w) \|_{H_2}^2 = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \| \tilde{A}_w^k \|_F^2 = \operatorname{Tr}(P),
\label{eq:hardy_lyapunov}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the unique, strictly positive definite solution ($P \succ 0$) to the discrete Lyapunov equation:
\begin{equation}
P - \tilde{A}_w^T P \tilde{A}_w = I_n.
\label{eq:lyapunov_eq}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Kato Perturbation Theory and Analytical Link Sensitivity Validation}
To analyze how localized infrastructure modifications (e.g., street closures, arterial lane additions, bus rapid transit reservations) alter urban spectral properties without re-running expensive matrix factorizations, we apply Kato\'s perturbation theory \cite{Kato1995}. Let $\tilde{A}_w(\epsilon) = \tilde{A}_w + \epsilon E_{ij}$ represent a localized capacity perturbation on directed edge $(v_i, v_j)$. The first- and second-order directional derivatives of the $k$-th eigenvalue $\lambda_k$ are analytically given by:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_k^{(1)} = \frac{d\lambda_k}{d\epsilon} = \frac{\mathbf{w}_k^T E_{ij} \mathbf{v}_k}{\mathbf{w}_k^T \mathbf{v}_k},
\label{eq:kato_1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\lambda_k^{(2)} = \frac{d^2\lambda_k}{d\epsilon^2} = \sum_{m \neq k} \frac{(\mathbf{w}_k^T E_{ij} \mathbf{v}_m)(\mathbf{w}_m^T E_{ij} \mathbf{v}_k)}{(\lambda_k - \lambda_m)(\mathbf{w}_k^T \mathbf{v}_k)(\mathbf{w}_m^T \mathbf{v}_m)},
\label{eq:kato_2}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_k, \mathbf{w}_k$ are the right and left eigenvectors of $\tilde{A}_w$ corresponding to $\lambda_k$. 

\textit{Numerical Validation Benchmark}:
Evaluating localized edge perturbations via the second-order Taylor approximation $\lambda_k(\epsilon) \approx \lambda_k(0) + \epsilon \lambda_k^{(1)} + \frac{\epsilon^2}{2} \lambda_k^{(2)}$ requires only $0.41$~ms per edge, compared to $89.4$~ms for a full ARPACK Arnoldi eigensolver recomputation ($218\times$ speedup) while maintaining a relative spectral approximation error below $0.12\%$.

\section{Surrogate Machine Learning Pipeline and Feature Engineering}

\subsection{Explanatory Feature Families (47 Descriptors)}
To capture the multi-physics coupling between network topology, non-normal operator spectrum, traffic demand, and fleet characteristics, we construct a 47-dimensional descriptor vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{47}$ structured into five functional families:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Kinematic Traffic Demand (4 Descriptors)}: Total simulated vehicle volume $N_{\text{veh}}$, mean vehicle dwell time $\bar{T}_{\text{sim}}$, total vehicle-kilometers traveled $L_{\text{tot}} = \sum_k \int_0^{T_k} \|\mathbf{v}_k(t)\| dt$, and network-wide mean legal speed limit $\bar{v}_{\text{free}} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{e \in E} W_e$.
    \item \textbf{Fleet Powertrain and Fuel Mix (2 Descriptors)}: Fleet electrification ratio $\text{Fleet}_{\text{elec}} = N_{\text{EV}} / N_{\text{veh}} \in [0, 1]$ (zero-tailpipe emissions) and heavy-duty freight fraction $\text{Fleet}_{\text{HGV}} = N_{\text{HGV}} / N_{\text{veh}} \in [0, 1]$.
    \item \textbf{Physical Road Network Topology (10 Descriptors)}: Intersection count $n$, segment count $m$, network density $\delta = m / (n(n-1))$, mean node degree $\bar{k}$ and degree standard deviation $\sigma_k$, mean edge length $\bar{L}$, total network length $L_{\text{net}} = \sum_{ij} L_{ij}$, speed limit variance $\sigma_W^2$, total lane storage capacity $C_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{ij} C_{ij}$, route circuity $\chi$, and planar meshedness coefficient $M = (m - n + 1) / (2n - 5)$.
    \item \textbf{Non-Normal Graph-Spectral Invariants (20 Descriptors)}: 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Unweighted and weighted Perron-Frobenius spectral radii $\rho(A_0)$ and $\rho(A_w)$ from \eqref{eq:adjacency} (2 descriptors).
        \item Spectral gap $\Delta\lambda = |\lambda_1(\tilde{A}_0) - \lambda_2(\tilde{A}_0)|$, where $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \in \mathbb{C}$ are the top 2 leading eigenvalues of $\tilde{A}_0$ sorted by descending modulus (1 descriptor).
        \item Top-5 leading eigenvalue moduli $|\lambda_1(\tilde{A}_w)|, \dots, |\lambda_5(\tilde{A}_w)|$ of the impedance-weighted transition matrix $\tilde{A}_w$ defined in \eqref{eq:normalized_A} (5 descriptors).
        \item Commutator non-normality norms $\Delta(\tilde{A}_0)$ and $\Delta(\tilde{A}_w)$ from \eqref{eq:commutator} (2 descriptors).
        \item Kreiss transient growth constants $K(\tilde{A}_0)$ and $K(\tilde{A}_w)$ from \eqref{eq:kreiss} (2 descriptors).
        \item Discrete Hardy $H_2$ resolvent norms $\|R(z, \tilde{A}_0)\|_{H_2}$ and $\|R(z, \tilde{A}_w)\|_{H_2}$ from \eqref{eq:hardy_lyapunov} (2 descriptors).
        \item $\epsilon$-pseudospectral radii $\alpha_{0.01}(\tilde{A}_0)$ and $\alpha_{0.01}(\tilde{A}_w)$ from \eqref{eq:pseudospectrum} with $\epsilon = 0.01$ (2 descriptors).
        \item First- and second-order Kato perturbation derivatives $\lambda^{(1)}(A_0), \lambda^{(2)}(A_0), \lambda^{(1)}(A_w), \lambda^{(2)}(A_w)$ defined in \eqref{eq:kato_1}--\eqref{eq:kato_2} (4 descriptors).
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Non-Linear Cross Interactions (11 Descriptors)}: Physically motivated cross-ratios capturing non-linear phase transitions: demand density $N_{\text{veh}} / L_{\text{net}}$, lane capacity saturation $N_{\text{veh}} / C_{\text{tot}}$, Spectral Shock Index $N_{\text{veh}} \cdot K(\tilde{A}_w) / C_{\text{tot}}$, Net ICE volume $N_{\text{veh}} \cdot (1 - \text{Fleet}_{\text{elec}})$, Non-Normal Shearing Load $N_{\text{veh}} \cdot \Delta(\tilde{A}_0) / n$, Kinetic Dissipation Index $\rho(A_w) \cdot \sigma_W / \bar{v}_{\text{free}}$, Transient Queue Load $N_{\text{veh}} \cdot K(\tilde{A}_w) / n$, Dynamic Dwell Shock $\bar{T}_{\text{sim}} \cdot \rho(A_w) / L_{\text{net}}$, Meshed Demand Capacity $M \cdot N_{\text{veh}} / C_{\text{tot}}$, Stochastic Resolvent Energy $\|R(z, \tilde{A}_w)\|_{H_2} \cdot N_{\text{veh}} / L_{\text{net}}$, and Fleet Congestion Interaction $\text{Fleet}_{\text{HGV}} \cdot N_{\text{veh}} / C_{\text{tot}}$.
\end{enumerate}

\textit{Analytical Commentary on Descriptor Synergy}:
The 47 descriptors systematically bridge micro-kinematics and macro-topology ($4 + 2 + 10 + 20 + 11 = 47$). While Family 1 and 2 define boundary throughput and emissions factors, Family 4 (Non-Normal Spectrum) captures how directed bottleneck asymmetries propagate congestion shockwaves across the entire network. Family 5 combines these features into dimensionless indices that allow XGBoost decision trees to accurately isolate non-linear traffic phase transitions.

\subsection{Target Normalization and Scale Decoupling}
In naive regression models targeting raw total carbon dioxide emissions $Y = \text{CO}_2$ (kg), the strong collinearity between vehicle volume and total fuel consumption ($r(N_{\text{veh}}, \text{CO}_2) = 0.9865$) causes optimization algorithms to allocate almost all split importance to $N_{\text{veh}}$. Consequently, topological and spectral features are suppressed as minor residuals, leading to catastrophic failure when transferring to cities with identical vehicle counts but radically different bottleneck topologies.

To eliminate scale confounding, we formulate the machine learning surrogate on the normalized per-vehicle target:
\begin{equation}
y = \frac{\text{CO}_2}{N_{\text{veh}}} \quad (\text{kg } \text{CO}_2 / \text{vehicle}).
\label{eq:normalized_target}
\end{equation}

Total urban emissions are reconstructed during inference via the deterministic identity:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\text{CO}}_2 = \hat{y} \cdot N_{\text{veh}} = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \cdot N_{\text{veh}}.
\label{eq:reconstructed_target}
\end{equation}

\subsection{XGBoost Formulation, Objective, and Hyperparameter Optimization}
We employ eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) \cite{Chen2016}, which constructs an ensemble of $K$ regression trees $\hat{y}_i = \sum_{k=1}^K f_k(\mathbf{x}_i)$ by minimizing the regularized objective:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \ell(y_i, \hat{y}_i) + \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \gamma T_k + \frac{1}{2} \lambda \| w_k \|^2 + \alpha \| w_k \|_1 \right),
\label{eq:xgboost_obj}
\end{equation}
where $\ell(y_i, \hat{y}_i) = (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ is the mean squared error loss, $T_k$ is the number of terminal leaves, $w_k$ is the leaf weight vector, $\gamma$ penalizes tree complexity, and $\lambda, \alpha$ provide $L_2$ and $L_1$ regularization.

\subsection{Zero-Shot Cross-City Generalization \& Convex Barycentric Diagnostic}
To evaluate model capability when deployed in a new city without any prior SUMO simulation calibration, we conduct a zero-shot validation across nine completely unobserved global metropolises: Amsterdam, Bogota, Casablanca, Dublin, Helsinki, Montreal, Seoul, Singapore, and Zurich.

To quantify whether an unseen city network lies within the topological interpolation domain of our training corpus, we formulate a 21-dimensional convex quadratic programming barycentric projection. The full 47-dimensional descriptor vector decomposes into $\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{demand}}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{fleet}}, \mathbf{x}_{\text{cross}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{47}$, where $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{21}$ isolates the normalized scale-invariant topological and spectral features ($n, m, \delta, \bar{k}, \sigma_k, \bar{L}, L_{\text{net}}, \sigma_W^2, C_{\text{tot}}, M, \rho(A_0), \rho(A_w), \Delta\lambda, |\lambda_1|, \dots, |\lambda_5|, \Delta(A_0), \Delta(A_w), K(A_w)$). Let $\mathbf{X}_{\text{train}} \in \mathbb{R}^{65 \times 21}$ denote the matrix of training cities in this invariant subspace. We solve:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{65}} \quad & \| \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{X}_{\text{train}}^T \mathbf{w} \|_2^2 + \gamma \| \mathbf{w} \|_2^2 \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \sum_{j=1}^{65} w_j = 1, \quad w_j \ge 0 \quad \forall j \in \{1, \dots, 65\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:barycentric_qp}
\end{equation}

Here, $n_{\text{active}} \in [1, 13]$ is defined explicitly as the count of training cities with strictly positive barycentric weights ($w_j > 0$). The residual distance to the training convex hull $d_{\mathcal{H}} = \| \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{X}_{\text{train}}^T \mathbf{w}^* \|_2$ provides an automated confidence diagnostic for zero-shot deployment.

Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot} presents the zero-shot validation results across all nine unseen cities.

\begin{table*}[htbp]

As reported in Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot}, Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot} reports 9 representative peak-hour base scenarios across the unseen test cities (achieving $R^2 = 99.67\%$, $\text{RMSE} = 3,199$~kg, $\text{MAPE} = 2.67\%$ across these 9 base runs), while the aggregate multi-scenario zero-shot benchmark evaluated across all 18 independent test runs spanning varying demand loads and EV penetrations yields $R^2 = 88.66\%$, $\text{RMSE} = 4,812$~kg, and $\text{MAPE} = 4.82\%$. This validates the convex barycentric distance $d_{\mathcal{H}}$ as an effective automated confidence metric for zero-shot deployment.

\subsection{Computational Latency and Digital Twin Integration}
For real-time municipal traffic control, computational latency is critical. We distinguish two operational regimes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Warm Start (Scenario Evaluation)}: The city graph and its spectral descriptors have been pre-computed. Evaluating a new traffic scenario (varying $N_{\text{veh}}$, $\text{Fleet}_{\text{elec}}$, $\bar{v}_{\text{free}}$, or edge capacities via Kato perturbation) requires only feature assembly and XGBoost tree traversal, executing in under $6$~milliseconds.
    \item \textbf{Cold Start (New City Onboarding)}: For a completely new metropolis, the system downloads OSM data via Overpass API, builds the graph, computes eigenvalues via sparse ARPACK Arnoldi iteration, and generates all 47 features.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Future Research Perspectives}

While the graph-spectral surrogate demonstrates high predictive accuracy and computational speedups exceeding $1.0 \times 10^6 \times$, three primary limitations define clear avenues for future research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Traffic Signal Timing Integration}: Currently, traffic signals are modeled via capacity derating factors embedded in edge impedance weights ($A_w$). Integrating dynamic signal actuation policies (e.g., adaptive split-cycle offset optimization \cite{Robertson1991, Hunt1982}) into time-varying non-normal operators $A_w(t)$ represents an important direction.
    \item \textbf{3D Cartography and Subterranean Topology}: As demonstrated by the Guanajuato case study, 2D planar OSM graphs fail in multi-level, tunnel-heavy topographies. Future iterations will incorporate 3D elevation meshes and grade-separated vertical connectivity into the adjacency tensor.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Pollutant Co-Surrogate Modeling}: While this work focuses on $\text{CO}_2$ greenhouse gas emissions, extending the multi-physics spectral framework to predict localized nitrogen oxides ($\text{NO}_x$) and particulate matter ($\text{PM}_{10}, \text{PM}_{2.5}$) dispersion represents an ongoing research initiative.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This paper introduced a physics-grounded graph-spectral surrogate framework for real-time estimation of urban traffic $\text{CO}_2$ emissions. By formulating directed urban road networks as asymmetric, dissipative transition operators ($\tilde{A}_0, \tilde{A}_w$) with spectral radius $\rho(\tilde{A}) \le 0.95 < 1$, we established mathematical convergence of discrete Hardy $H_2$ resolvent spaces and discrete Lyapunov equations while extracting 20 non-normal spectral invariants, including the Perron-Frobenius spectral radius $\rho(A)$, the Kreiss transient growth constant $K(\tilde{A})$, commutator non-normality $\Delta(\tilde{A})$, $\epsilon$-pseudospectral radii $\alpha_\epsilon(\tilde{A})$, and analytical Kato perturbation derivatives.

Combined with traffic demand, physical topology, and fleet electrification into a 47-dimensional descriptor vector, our vehicle-normalized XGBoost surrogate ($y = \text{CO}_2 / N_{\text{veh}}$) was trained and evaluated on 349 microscopic SUMO simulations across 65 global cities. The framework achieved an internal test $R^2 = 89.39\%$ on normalized emissions, $R^2 = 98.95\%$ on rebuilt total emissions, and 5-fold cross-validation $R^2 = 97.99 \pm 0.90\%$, outperforming spatial GCNs by $+17.75$ percentage points. On nine unseen zero-shot validation cities, the model achieved $R^2 = 99.67\%$ on representative base cases ($R^2 = 88.66\%$ across the full multi-scenario evaluation cohort), supported by a 21-dimensional convex quadratic programming barycentric projection that quantifies topological confidence. With warm-start inference times under $6$~ms (speedup $> 1.0 \times 10^6 \times$ over SUMO) and cold-start onboarding under $132$~s, this surrogate provides an operational decision-support tool for municipal decarbonization and real-time digital twins.
